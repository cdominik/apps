\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

The \textbf{GT4Pebbles} (Ground Truth for Pebbles) project studies how planet
formation begins in protoplanetary disks around young stars. A central
experimental apparatus is a rotating drum in which dust particles are
levitated for extended periods, allowing them to collide and grow through a
sequence of stages: free particles $\to$ aggregates $\to$ compressed
aggregates---a prestage for pebble formation.

The \textbf{Levitation Lab} is a browser-based interactive simulation
that captures the core physics of this experiment. It is used as a
game, as an outreach tool---allowing members of the public to
explore the experiment, and as a pedagogical tool for students and
researchers. The simulation is built entirely in vanilla JavaScript
and HTML5 Canvas, with no external physics engine.

This document covers:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The underlying physical model and all governing equations.
  \item The numerical implementation, including timestep management and
        substep logic.
  \item Every user interface control, including button icons, with rationale
        for design choices.
  \item The game and challenge modes and their win/fail logic.
  \item The expert analytical panel and its visualisation tools.
  \item Audio, viewport, and URL parameter systems.
\end{itemize}

\section{Coordinate System and Drum Geometry}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Coordinate System}

The simulation uses a right-handed coordinate system centred on the drum axis,
with $x$ pointing right and $y$ pointing upward. Positions are measured in
\textbf{drum units} (equivalent to centimetres in the physical experiment).

The drum has inner radius $R = 100\,\mathrm{du}$ and a hub of radius
$R_{\mathrm{axis}} = 5\,\mathrm{du}$. The drum extrusion depth (into the
screen) is $20\,\mathrm{du}$, but this dimension is not used in the 2-D
simulation; it serves only as a conceptual reference.

Canvas rendering converts drum coordinates to screen pixels as:
\begin{align}
  x_{\mathrm{px}} &= C_x + x \cdot S, \\
  y_{\mathrm{px}} &= C_y - y \cdot S,
\end{align}
where $(C_x, C_y)$ is the screen-space drum centre and $S$ is the pixel scale
(pixels per drum unit), both computed by the layout engine from the window size.

\subsection{Levitation Zone}

The levitation zone (the \emph{highlight zone} in the code) is a disc centred
at $(\Hcx, H_{\mathrm{cy}}) = (50, 0)$ with radius $\Hr = 50\,\mathrm{du}$.
This places the zone on the right side of the drum, tangent to both the drum
wall and the axis. A particle is considered \emph{levitated} when it has
remained continuously inside the levitation zone for at least one full drum
revolution.

\section{Drum Rotation Dynamics}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Angular Velocity Model}

The drum has two angular velocity variables: the \emph{target} velocity
$\omg_{\mathrm{target}}$ (set by the user via swipe or auto-omega) and the
\emph{actual} velocity $\omg$.

The target decays exponentially each frame:
\begin{equation}
  \omg_{\mathrm{target}}(t + \Delta t) = \omg_{\mathrm{target}}(t)
    \cdot e^{-\lambda_d \Delta t},
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_d = 1/30\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ (i.e.\ a 30-second decay time
constant). This models aerodynamic drag on the drum once the user stops
spinning it. The decay can be disabled with the \textbf{$\Omega$ lock} button.

The actual velocity tracks the target with a slip rate
$\lambda_s = 0.7\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$:
\begin{equation}
  \omg(t + \Delta t) = \omg(t) + \bigl[\omg_{\mathrm{target}}(t+\Delta t)
    - \omg(t)\bigr] \cdot \bigl(1 - e^{-\lambda_s \Delta t}\bigr).
\end{equation}

\subsection{User Input: Swipe}

A pointer drag across the drum surface imparts angular momentum proportional
to the tangential component of the drag velocity:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\omg_{\mathrm{target}} = g_s \cdot
    \frac{\Delta s_{\mathrm{tang}}}{r / R},
\end{equation}
where $g_s = 0.003\,\mathrm{(rad\,s^{-1})/px}$ is the swipe gain,
$\Delta s_{\mathrm{tang}}$ is the pointer displacement projected onto the
tangential direction, and $r$ is the distance of the midpoint from the axis.
The denominator $(r/R)$ normalises for swipes near the axis versus near the
wall, giving consistent feel at all radii. The result is clamped to
$|\omg_{\mathrm{target}}| \leq \omg_{\max} = 2\pi \times 1.5\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$.

\paragraph{Design note.} The physical experiment runs at approximately
$1\,\mathrm{RPM} \approx 0.105\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$. At that speed,
the levitation feedback to the user would be far too slow for an interactive
experience. The simulation maximum of $1.5\,\mathrm{rev\,s^{-1}}$ is
approximately 90 times faster, but preserves all the qualitative physics.

\section{Particle Kinematics and Levitation}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Equations of Motion}

In the rotating frame, the gas co-rotates with the drum and exerts a drag force
on the particle that brings its velocity to the local gas velocity minus its
terminal settling velocity $\vt$. In the (non-rotating) lab frame, the
particle's instantaneous velocity is:
\begin{align}
  \dot{x} &= -\omg y, \label{eq:vx} \\
  \dot{y} &= +\omg x - \vt. \label{eq:vy}
\end{align}

\noindent Equation~\eqref{eq:vx}--\eqref{eq:vy} give the gas velocity at the
particle's position (a co-rotating body has $\dot{x} = -\omg y$,
$\dot{y} = +\omg x$), reduced by $\vt$ in the $y$-direction. The settling
velocity $\vt$ is always directed downward (the $-\hat{y}$ direction) and
represents the particle's Stokes terminal velocity in quiescent gas.

This is a \emph{kinematic} model: accelerations are not integrated; the
velocity is set to the equilibrium value at every substep. This is valid when
the aerodynamic drag time is much shorter than the orbital period, which is
the overdamped (high-Stokes-drag) limit appropriate for the small particles
of interest.

\subsection{Circular Orbits}

Setting $\ddot{x} = \ddot{y} = 0$ in the Newtonian sense (the orbit is
closed) gives the orbit centre:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\xc = \frac{\vt}{\omg}, \qquad y_c = 0.}
  \label{eq:orbit_centre}
\end{equation}

A particle at $(x_0, y_0)$ therefore orbits a circle of radius
\begin{equation}
  r = \sqrt{(x_0 - \xc)^2 + y_0^2}
\end{equation}
centred at $(\xc, 0)$. The orbit traces out at angular rate $\omg$ (the same
rate as the drum).

From eq.~\eqref{eq:orbit_centre}, heavier particles (larger $\vt$) orbit at
larger $\xc$ and therefore closer to the drum wall. For a particle to remain
levitated, its entire orbit must stay within the levitation zone. Since the
levitation zone is centred at $(\Hcx, 0) = (50, 0)$ with radius
$\Hr = 50$, this corresponds roughly to $\xc \approx 50$ and
$r \lesssim 30$--$40\,\mathrm{du}$.

\subsection{Terminal Velocity Distributions}

Each injection event draws $N_P$ particles with terminal velocities from one
of three distributions:

\subsubsection{Gaussian (default)}
\begin{equation}
  \vt^{(i)} = \bar{\vt} \cdot \bigl(1 + s \cdot \xi_i\bigr),
  \qquad \xi_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1),
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\vt}$ is the mean terminal velocity and $s$ is the relative spread
parameter (SPREAD slider, $0 \leq s \leq 0.5$).

\subsubsection{Bi-disperse}
Two groups with terminal velocities $\vt^{(1)}, \vt^{(2)}$, fractional
spreads $s_1, s_2$, and mixing ratio $q$ (particles in group 1 per particle
in group 2):
\begin{equation}
  P(\text{group 1}) = \frac{q}{1 + q}.
\end{equation}
Within each group, the terminal velocity is Gaussian with the group's own
$\bar{\vt}^{(k)}$ and $s_k$.

\subsubsection{Power-law}
The differential size distribution $n(v) \propto v^q$ on $[v_{\min}, v_{\max}]$
is sampled by inverse-transform:
\begin{equation}
  \vt = \Bigl[u\,\bigl(v_{\max}^{q+1} - v_{\min}^{q+1}\bigr)
    + v_{\min}^{q+1}\Bigr]^{1/(q+1)}, \qquad u \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1),
\end{equation}
with the special case $q = -1$ (log-uniform):
\begin{equation}
  \vt = v_{\min} \cdot \left(\frac{v_{\max}}{v_{\min}}\right)^u.
\end{equation}

In all cases, $\vt$ is clamped from below at the \emph{settle floor}
$= 2\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$.

\subsection{Visual Size}

The rendered radius of a particle scales as:
\begin{equation}
  s_{\mathrm{vis}} = \sqrt{\frac{\vt}{\vt^{\mathrm{ref}}}},
  \qquad
  \vt^{\mathrm{ref}} = 30\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}},
\end{equation}
clamped to $[s_{\min}, s_{\max}] = [0.55, 1.8]$ drum units, and further
scaled down by a factor $0.1$--$0.7$ at large particle counts
($N_P \geq 300$) to keep the display readable.

\subsection{Injection and Release}

Particles are released from a brass manifold above the drum
at $y = 110\,\mathrm{du}$ (above the wall), spread uniformly in $x$ across
$[0, 100]\,\mathrm{du}$. They fall freely (no drag) until they cross the inner
drum wall, at which point the kinematic drag model~\eqref{eq:vx}--\eqref{eq:vy}
activates. Injection events are evenly spaced over the injection interval
$\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj}}$; a puff animation accompanies the start of injection
(one puff per nozzle, no further puffs regardless of particle count, to avoid
GPU performance issues on mobile).

\subsection{Wall Collision and Particle Loss}

At each substep, if a non-stuck particle's distance from the axis satisfies
$r \geq R_{\mathrm{wall}} = R - r_{\mathrm{coll}}$ (where
$r_{\mathrm{coll}} = 0.5\,\mathrm{du}$ is the collision radius), the particle
is \emph{stuck}: it is fixed to the wall at the collision angle and rotates
with the drum. Stuck particles are displayed in red, fade over $5\,\mathrm{s}$,
and then removed. The \texttt{lostCount} accumulator is incremented.

With \textbf{Nanocoating} active, wall contact is handled elastically instead:
the radial component of the velocity is reflected, and the particle is clamped
just inside the wall.

\section{Automatic Drum Speed Control}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Orbit-Centring Formula}

The auto-omega function computes the drum speed that centres the range of orbit
centres for the current $\vt$ distribution inside the levitation zone.

From eq.~\eqref{eq:orbit_centre}, an orbit at $\vt$ is centred at
$\xc = \vt / \omg$. For a distribution spanning $[\vt^{\min}, \vt^{\max}]$,
the orbit centres span
$[\vt^{\min}/\omg,\; \vt^{\max}/\omg]$.
Centring this range on $\Hcx = 50$ gives:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\omg_{\mathrm{auto}} = \frac{\vt^{\min} + \vt^{\max}}{2 \Hcx}.}
  \label{eq:auto_omega}
\end{equation}

For the Gaussian distribution,
$\vt^{\min} = \bar{\vt}(1 - s)$, $\vt^{\max} = \bar{\vt}(1 + s)$, giving
$\omg_{\mathrm{auto}} = \bar{\vt}/\Hcx$, independent of spread.

For bi-disperse and power-law distributions, the full envelope of the
distribution is used. The result is clamped to $[0.02, \omg_{\max}]$.

\subsection{Launch Mode}

In \emph{launch mode} (three-state auto-omega button, second state), the
controller waits for particles to be injected, estimates the time of peak
particle flux at $y = 0$, spins the drum up just before that time using a
kernel-density estimate of the arrival time distribution, then transitions to
live tracking. The optimal spin-up time is:
\begin{equation}
  t_{\mathrm{wait}} = \hat{t}_{\mathrm{peak}} - \frac{1}{\lambda_s},
\end{equation}
where $\hat{t}_{\mathrm{peak}}$ is the KDE mode of arrival times
$t_{\mathrm{arr}}^{(i)} = t_{\mathrm{inj}}^{(i)} + R_{\mathrm{release}}/\vt^{(i)}$
and the subtracted term accounts for the drum slip lag.

\section{Aggregate Formation}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Formation Threshold and Trigger}

Aggregate formation becomes possible when at least
$N_{\mathrm{lev}}^{\min} = 30$ particles are simultaneously levitated and the
velocity spread satisfies $s \geq s_{\min}^{\mathrm{agg}} = 0.20$.

Once the threshold is met, a counter accumulates in units of drum revolutions:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{agg}} \mathrel{+}= \frac{\Delta t}{T},
  \qquad T = \frac{2\pi}{\omg}.
\end{equation}
The first aggregate forms after $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{agg}} \geq 3$
revolutions; each subsequent one after $\geq 1$ revolution (scaled down at
large $N_P$ to maintain visual interest).

\subsection{Merge Animation}

When triggered, $n_{\mathrm{merge}} = 10$ levitated particles near a randomly
chosen levitated particle are selected as the nearest cluster. They are animated
along straight paths toward their centroid over a duration
$\tau_{\mathrm{merge}} = 0.6\,\mathrm{s}$, then replaced by a single aggregate.

\subsection{Aggregate Terminal Velocity and Stokes Kick}

The aggregate's terminal velocity is the mass-weighted mean of its constituent
monomers, scaled by a Stokes-number factor:
\begin{equation}
  \vt^{\mathrm{agg}} = \bar{\vt} \cdot f_{\mathrm{Stokes}},
  \qquad f_{\mathrm{Stokes}} = 1.2 \text{ (when Stokes kick is on)}.
\end{equation}

The factor $f_{\mathrm{Stokes}} > 1$ reflects two physical effects acting in
the same direction:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Increased inertia}: a compact aggregate has a higher
    Stokes number (ratio of aerodynamic response time to orbital period) than
    an individual monomer, so it settles faster in the rotating gas frame.
  \item \textbf{Cross-section shadowing}: the aggregate's projected area is
    smaller than the sum of its constituent areas (for compact, roughly spherical
    aggregates), reducing drag per unit mass.
\end{enumerate}

The effect is that aggregates orbit at a slightly larger $\xc$ than the
monomer population from which they formed, enabling them to orbit closer to the
wall and reducing the risk of further levitation loss.

When \textbf{Stokes kick} is off, $f_{\mathrm{Stokes}} = 1$ and the aggregate
orbits exactly at the mean monomer orbit centre.

\subsection{Aggregate Orbital Mechanics}

Aggregates follow the same kinematic orbit equations
\eqref{eq:vx}--\eqref{eq:vy} as monomers, but with their own $\vt^{\mathrm{agg}}$.
Additionally, when \textbf{aggregate growth mode} is active, turbulent
perturbations are applied each substep:
\begin{align}
  \delta v_x &= \sigma_B \xi_x, \label{eq:brownian_x} \\
  \delta v_y &= \sigma_B \xi_y, \label{eq:brownian_y}
\end{align}
where $\xi_{x,y} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and $\sigma_B = 2\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$.
A restoring force returns the aggregate toward its natural orbit centre
$(\xc, 0)$:
\begin{align}
  \dot{v}_x^{\mathrm{restore}} &= k_R (\xc - x), \\
  \dot{v}_y^{\mathrm{restore}} &= k_R (0 - y),
\end{align}
with spring constant $k_R = 0.5\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$. This is a Langevin-type
equation that produces a random walk around the orbit centre with a
characteristic diffusion length set by $\sigma_B / k_R$.

The perturbations cause aggregate orbits to drift, enabling close approaches
with other aggregates.

\subsection{Aggregate--Aggregate Growth Collisions}

When growth mode is active (\btnicon{btnProcGrowth} orange state), aggregate
pairs are checked for overlap each frame. When two aggregates touch
($d < r_i + r_j$), the smaller merges into the larger over
$\tau_{\mathrm{grow}} = 0.6\,\mathrm{s}$:
\begin{align}
  n_{\mathrm{new}} &= n_i + n_j, \\
  \vt^{\mathrm{new}} &= \frac{n_i \vt^{(i)} + n_j \vt^{(j)}}{n_i + n_j}
    \cdot f_{\mathrm{grow}},
\end{align}
where $f_{\mathrm{grow}} = 1.05$ per growth step (when Stokes kick is on).
The visual radius grows as:
\begin{equation}
  r_{\mathrm{agg}} = r_{\mathrm{coll}} \cdot s_{\mathrm{vis}} \cdot
    n_{\mathrm{mult}} \cdot \sqrt{n/10},
\end{equation}
with $n_{\mathrm{mult}} = 10$ (the aggregate size multiplier) and $n$ the
current monomer count.

An aggregate reaching $n \geq 100$ monomers automatically becomes a
\textbf{pebble} (golden ball).

\subsection{Wall Collision of Aggregates}

An aggregate reaching the drum wall fragments into 10 stuck monomers spread
around the impact angle, and the aggregate count is decremented. With
\textbf{Nanocoating} active, the aggregate is instead elastically reflected.

\section{Pebble (Golden Ball) Formation}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Formation Threshold}

After $n_{\mathrm{agg}}^{\mathrm{crit}} = 7$ aggregates are simultaneously
levitated for a hold time of $H_{\mathrm{pebble}} = 3$ revolutions (first
pebble) or $3$ revolutions (subsequent), a pebble merge is triggered. The
velocity spread condition $s \geq s_{\min}^{\mathrm{pebble}} = 0.30$ must
also be satisfied.

The aggregate targets are chosen by a K-nearest-neighbours search
($K = 3$) to find the densest cluster, producing visually coherent merges.
The $n_{\mathrm{agg}}^{\mathrm{crit}} = 7$ chosen aggregates are animated
toward their centroid over $\tau_{\mathrm{pebble}} = 0.6\,\mathrm{s}$, and
the aggregate count is reduced by 7 on completion.

\subsection{Golden Ball Physics}

Golden balls obey Newtonian dynamics, not the kinematic drag model:
\begin{align}
  \ddot{x} &= 0, \\
  \ddot{y} &= -g = -200\,\mathrm{du\,s^{-2}}.
\end{align}

They bounce off the drum wall with restitution coefficient
$e_w = 0.85$ (normal) and tangential friction $\mu_w = 0.92$:
\begin{align}
  v_n^+ &= -e_w v_n^-, \\
  v_t^+ &= \mu_w v_t^-,
\end{align}
where superscripts $\pm$ denote post- and pre-collision components.

Ball--ball collisions are handled with coefficient $e_b = 0.90$:
\begin{equation}
  J = \frac{(1 + e_b)\,v_{\mathrm{rel,n}}}{2},
  \qquad \Delta\mathbf{v}_{A,B} = \mp J\,\hat{n}.
\end{equation}

A rotating \textbf{bump} fixed to the drum wall (at the same angular position
as the collection tray slot) collides with balls as the drum rotates, adding
energy to keep them bouncing. The bump collision applies a fractional impulse
controlled by \texttt{ball.bumpStrength} $= 0.1$; a value of 0 makes the bump
a ghost and 1 gives a fully elastic kick.

Wall contact drag interpolates the ball's tangential velocity toward the
drum surface velocity with time constant $\lambda_c = 0.7\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$.

% =============================================================================
\section{Globe (Planet) Formation}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Formation Threshold}

When $n_{\mathrm{ball}}^{\mathrm{crit}} = 3$ golden balls are simultaneously
present, they merge into a \textbf{globe} over $\tau_{\mathrm{globe}} = 1.2\,\mathrm{s}$.
Up to 7 globes can exist simultaneously.

\subsection{Globe Hover Physics}

Each globe is attracted toward the hover point $(0, -50)$ (drum units) by a
spring:
\begin{align}
  \dot{v}_x &\mathrel{+}= 0.01 \cdot 60 \cdot (0 - x)\,\Delta t, \\
  \dot{v}_y &\mathrel{+}= 0.01 \cdot 60 \cdot (-50 - y)\,\Delta t,
\end{align}
and repelled from all other globes by a $1/r^2$ force with constant
$F_{\mathrm{rep}} = 15000\,\mathrm{du^3\,s^{-2}}$, with an additional linear
hard-contact push when $d < 2.2 R_{\mathrm{globe}}$.

Velocity is damped at $\exp(-9.74\,\Delta t)$ per frame, equivalent to
$\approx 0.85$ per frame at $60\,\mathrm{fps}$.

% =============================================================================
\section{Solar System Phase Machine}
% =============================================================================

Once a second globe forms, the simulation enters a solar system mode and
progresses through a finite state machine: \texttt{showing}
$\to$ \texttt{transitioning} $\to$ \texttt{orbiting}
$\to$ \texttt{spindown} $\to$ \texttt{final\_move}
$\to$ \texttt{final\_view}.

\subsection{Orbital Dynamics}

Each globe is assigned an orbit with semi-major axis from the sequence
$[11, 17, 24, 31, 37, 42, 50]\,\mathrm{du}$ (scaled dynamically to fit all
globes inside the drum). The orbital angular velocity follows a modified
Keplerian law:
\begin{equation}
  \omg_{\mathrm{orbit}}(r) = \frac{\omg_0}{r^{0.75}},
  \qquad \omg_0 \approx 3.006\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}},
\end{equation}
where $\omg_0 = (2\pi/8) \cdot 6^{0.75}$.

The exponent $0.75$ is shallower than the true Keplerian value of $1.5$.
This is a deliberate design choice: the full Keplerian scaling would make inner
planets orbit extremely fast relative to outer ones, which looks chaotic at the
speed of the simulation. The $0.75$ exponent gives a visually convincing
impression of varying orbital periods while keeping all orbits visible at once.

The orbits are displayed as inclined ellipses with inclination
$\cos i = 0.3$ (i.e.\ $i \approx 73^{\circ}$), giving a sense of a tilted orbital
plane viewed in 3-D perspective.

\subsection{System Scale}

As new globes are added, the system scale $\mathcal{S}$ is reduced so that
all orbits fit within the drum:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{S}_n = \frac{R_{\max}}{r_n + r_{\mathrm{orb}}},
\end{equation}
where $R_{\max} = 80\,\mathrm{du}$ is the maximum safe orbit radius,
$r_n$ is the outermost orbit radius for $n$ globes, and
$r_{\mathrm{orb}} = 3\,\mathrm{du}$ is the visual orbit size.
Transitions between scales are eased with a cubic smoothstep over
$3\,\mathrm{s}$.

\subsection{Voyager Probes}

After the solar system reaches its final static state (all seven planets,
drum stopped), two probe objects are launched sequentially (with a $2.4\,\mathrm{s}$
gap) from the innermost planet. Each probe visits all planets in order at
speed $28\,\mathrm{du\,s^{-1}}$, then escapes radially to the drum edge.
The probes leave luminous trails. When both probes have completed their
escape, an end-of-game sheet is shown after a $2\,\mathrm{s}$ delay.

\section{Numerical Implementation}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Timestep and Substep Logic}

The main loop runs at the browser's animation frame rate
($\approx 60\,\mathrm{fps}$ on most displays). The wall-clock frame duration
$\Delta t_{\mathrm{frame}}$ is capped at $\Delta t_{\max} = 0.033\,\mathrm{s}$
to prevent large jumps after tab switches or garbage collection pauses.
A simulation speed multiplier $s_{\mathrm{sim}} \in [0.25, 4]$ is applied:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta t = \min(\Delta t_{\mathrm{frame}},\, \Delta t_{\max}) \cdot s_{\mathrm{sim}}.
\end{equation}

Physics are advanced in substeps of size $h = \Delta t / n_{\mathrm{sub}}$,
where:
\begin{equation}
  n_{\mathrm{sub}} = \max\Bigl(1,\; \left\lceil\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta t_{\mathrm{sub}}}\right\rceil\Bigr),
  \qquad \Delta t_{\mathrm{sub}} = \frac{T_{\min}}{100}
    = \frac{2\pi/\omg_{\max}}{100}.
\end{equation}

At $\omg_{\max} = 2\pi \times 1.5\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$,
$\Delta t_{\mathrm{sub}} \approx 6.7\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s}$, giving
$\approx 10$ substeps per frame at $60\,\mathrm{fps}$. This ensures that even
the fastest orbiting particles do not cross the collision margin in a single
substep.

\subsection{Slow-Motion During Merges}

When the slow-motion flag is armed (\btnicon{btnProcSlowMo}), any frame
during which a merge animation is active (aggregate, pebble, or globe) is
advanced at $1/5$ real speed:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta t \to \Delta t / 5 \quad \text{(when merging)}.
\end{equation}
This does not affect the audio system, which runs on the Web Audio API and
is independent of the simulation clock.

\subsection{Stroboscopic Mode}

When strobe mode is active (\btnicon{btnSysStrobe}), the canvas is redrawn
only once per complete drum revolution. The revolution counter is tracked as
$\lfloor |\theta_{\mathrm{drum}}| / 2\pi \rfloor$; when this advances, a
redraw is triggered. Levitated particles appear stationary; drifting particles
advance between flashes, revealing their motion.

\subsection{Simulation Speed Gears}

Five speed gears are available: $\{0.25\times, 0.5\times, 1\times, 2\times,
4\times\}$. The multiplier applies to $\Delta t$ before substep splitting.
The drum decay, injection timing, particle injection, and all physics scale
with the simulation clock, so all processes are accelerated or decelerated
uniformly.

\subsection{Pause}

Pressing \texttt{Space} pauses the simulation. All physics, drum rotation,
and motor audio freeze; the canvas continues to render the frozen state.

\section{Heatmap and Analytical Overlays}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Heatmap Accumulation}

The drum interior is divided into a $50 \times 50$ grid. Each substep, each
live non-stuck particle is binned by position, and three quantities are
accumulated per cell:
$n$ (count), $\Sigma v$ (speed sum), and $\Sigma v^2$ (speed-squared sum).

At the end of each complete drum revolution, the following quantities are
finalised for each cell:
\begin{align}
  \bar{v} &= \frac{\Sigma v}{n}, \\
  \sigma_v &= \sqrt{\frac{\Sigma v^2}{n} - \bar{v}^2}
    \quad \text{(velocity dispersion)}, \\
  \rho &= \frac{n}{N_{\mathrm{ticks}}}
    \quad \text{(mean particle density per frame)}, \\
  \mathcal{C} &= \rho \cdot \sigma_v^2
    \quad \text{(collision proxy)}.
\end{align}

The collision proxy $\mathcal{C} = \rho \sigma_v^2$ is motivated by the
kinetic theory expression for the collision rate between two particle
populations: $R_{12} \propto n_1 n_2 \sigma_{\mathrm{rel}}$, where
$\sigma_{\mathrm{rel}} \propto \sigma_v$ for particles with different $\vt$.
The proxy therefore highlights regions where both population density and
velocity dispersion are high---i.e.\ where aggregate formation is most likely.

Three display modes cycle through with the $\blacktriangleleft$ $\blacktriangleright$ buttons:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Velocity variance} $\sigma_v$ --- shows where particles have
    the largest spread in speed, indicating different orbital radii crossing.
  \item \textbf{Particle density} $\rho$ --- shows where particles spend the
    most time.
  \item \textbf{Collision proxy} $\mathcal{C} = \rho \sigma_v^2$ --- combines
    the two, highlighting the most collision-active region.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Orbit Projections}

The \emph{selected orbits} mode draws, for a representative sample of particles:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The orbit circle centred at $(\xc, 0) = (\vt/\omg, 0)$ with
    radius $r = \sqrt{(x-\xc)^2 + y^2}$.
  \item A dot marking $\xc$ on the $x$-axis.
  \item Green circles for levitated particles, red for non-levitated.
\end{itemize}
The sample is updated every $3\,\mathrm{s}$ and comprises: the 5
smallest-circumference orbits, up to 10 levitated particles spanning the
full $\xc$ range, and up to 10 non-levitated particles spanning their $\xc$
range.

\subsection{Aggregate Encounter Prediction}

For each pair of live aggregates, the simulation analytically computes the
\emph{closest approach} position during the next orbit. Each aggregate
follows a circular orbit:
\begin{align}
  x_i(\theta) &= \xc^{(i)} + R_i \cos(\phi_i + \theta), \\
  y_i(\theta) &= R_i \sin(\phi_i + \theta),
\end{align}
where $\theta$ is the orbital phase advance (both advance at the same rate
$\omg$). The squared separation is:
\begin{equation}
  d^2(\theta) = C_0 + 2\,\Delta\xc\,(P\cos\theta - Q\sin\theta),
\end{equation}
where
$P = R_i\cos\phi_i - R_j\cos\phi_j$,
$Q = R_i\sin\phi_i - R_j\sin\phi_j$,
$\Delta\xc = \xc^{(i)} - \xc^{(j)}$.
The minimum is at $\theta^* = \arctan(-Q, P)$; both $\theta^*$ and
$\theta^* + \pi$ are evaluated and the smaller $d^2$ is taken.

The top 10 closest-approach pairs are shown with connecting lines. When a
pair is within $\Delta\theta \approx 0.15\,\mathrm{rad}$ of their closest
approach, the display flashes white.

\subsection{Ghost Paths}

In ghost mode, a selected particle's future trajectory is shown in three
versions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{White solid}: actual kinematic orbit
    (integrating eqs.~\eqref{eq:vx}--\eqref{eq:vy} for 40 substeps of
    $0.05\,\mathrm{s}$).
  \item \textbf{Cyan dashed}: gas-only co-rotation (no settling, i.e.\
    $\vt = 0$).
  \item \textbf{Purple dashed}: free-fall vacuum trajectory
    (no drag, only gravity $g = 200\,\mathrm{du\,s^{-2}}$ downward).
\end{itemize}
The comparison between the three paths makes the role of aerodynamic drag
and the settling velocity physically intuitive.

\subsection{$v_t$ Distribution Display}

Live kernel-density estimates are drawn for:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Yellow}: $\vt$ distribution of all floating (non-levitated) particles.
  \item \textbf{Green}: $\vt$ distribution of levitated particles.
\end{itemize}
The KDE uses Silverman's rule of thumb for bandwidth:
$h = 1.06\,\hat\sigma\,n^{-1/5}$.
An amber histogram shows $\vt$ values of live aggregates.

All three are normalised independently (height = 1 at peak), so relative
shapes are directly comparable regardless of population size.

\subsection{Aggregate Size Histogram}

A bar chart inside the left half of the drum shows the current aggregate
population binned by monomer count in steps of 10 (bins 10--100), plus a
gold bar for pebbles. The $y$-axis is fixed at a maximum of 10; count
labels appear above bars when counts exceed the axis. The display updates
continuously.

\section{Collection Tray}
% =============================================================================

The collection tray is a retractable metal blade that can be inserted into
the drum to capture levitated particles, aggregates, and pebbles.

\subsection{Arming}

Pressing the \textbf{Collect} button (\btnicon{btnCollect}) arms the tray.
The arm waits for the gold slot marker on the bolt ring to cross the
12-o'clock position on screen (canvas angle $-\pi/2$). At that moment,
insertion begins. Pressing the button again while armed cancels arming.

\subsection{Insertion Kinematics}

The blade extends from the slot marker (the hinge point on the rim) inward
along a fixed direction locked at fire time. The direction aims toward a
point $(0.447R, 0.224R)$ in drum coordinates, producing a blade that sweeps
a chord roughly $0.89R$ long.

Insertion sweeps $240°$ ($= 4\pi/3\,\mathrm{rad}$) of drum rotation,
divided into two phases:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Cruise} (first $2/3$ of travel, i.e.\ $160°$): drum speed
    held constant at the firing value.
  \item \textbf{Brake} (final $1/3$, i.e.\ $80°$): drum decelerates smoothly
    to zero via a cubic smoothstep ramp:
    \begin{equation}
      \omg(t) = \omg_0 \cdot \bigl(1 - 3u^2 + 2u^3\bigr),
      \quad u = (t - t_{\mathrm{brake}}) / \tau_{\mathrm{brake}}.
    \end{equation}
    The brake duration $\tau_{\mathrm{brake}}$ is computed at fire time from
    the drum's actual angular velocity:
    \begin{equation}
      \tau_{\mathrm{brake}} = \frac{2(1-d_s)\,\Phi_{\mathrm{total}}}{|\omg_0|},
    \end{equation}
    where $d_s = 0.667$ is the decel-start fraction and
    $\Phi_{\mathrm{total}} = 4\pi/3$. This ensures the brake phase always
    covers exactly $1/3$ of the total sweep regardless of initial speed.
    (\texttt{TUNING.tray.brakeDur} exists in the config for reference but is
    not used at runtime; the value is computed dynamically as above.)
\end{enumerate}

Particles and aggregates within $2 \times \tau_{\mathrm{thick}}$ of the tray
line are captured and pinned to the upper blade surface, then co-rotate with
the drum until insertion is complete.

\section{Slice Widening (Doubling)}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Physical Justification}

The simulation models a single axial \emph{slice} of the drum. Early in a
run, this slice is injected with $N_P$ particles. Through wall losses and
aggregate/pebble formation, the active particle count decreases. Computational
capacity is then underused.

The physical assumption is that adjacent axial slices have undergone the same
process: they started with a similar particle distribution and have evolved
identically (same levitation conditions, same statistics). Widening the
simulated slice to include one adjacent slice should therefore approximately
double the levitated population without introducing any particles that are
physically implausible.

\subsection{Implementation}

Pressing \btnicon{btnProcDouble} duplicates all currently levitated particles
and all currently levitated aggregates. Each copy is placed $90°$ ahead of
the original in its orbit. The copy's position is computed as:
\begin{align}
  x' &= \xc + r \cos(\phi + \pi/2) = \xc - r\sin\phi = \xc - y, \\
  y' &= r \sin(\phi + \pi/2) = r\cos\phi = x - \xc,
\end{align}
and the copy's velocity is set to the orbital velocity at the new position:
\begin{align}
  v_x' &= -\omg y', \\
  v_y' &= +\omg x' - \vt.
\end{align}
The $90°$ offset ensures no two copies are at the same position, avoiding
initialisation artefacts. The copies inherit \texttt{inHighlightSince} from
the originals so they count as immediately levitated. The button flashes
without action if the drum is not spinning (orbits are undefined).

\section{Challenge Mode}
\label{sec:challenge}
% =============================================================================

Challenge mode runs a timed levitation contest with a weighted score:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Score} = N_{\mathrm{part}} + 10\,N_{\mathrm{agg}}
    + 100\,N_{\mathrm{pebble}} + 1000\,N_{\mathrm{planet}},
\end{equation}
where each count is taken at run end. A particle counts if it has been
continuously levitated for $\geq 3$ full revolutions.

The run ends when all injected particles are either levitated, stuck, or lost,
or when the time limit $\Delta t_{\mathrm{limit}} = 60 + \Delta t_{\mathrm{inj}}$
seconds is exceeded.

Default challenge parameters: $N_P = 300$, $\vt = 30\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$,
$s = 0.30$, $\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj}} = 3\,\mathrm{s}$. All can be overridden
by URL parameters (see §\ref{sec:url}).

Scores are stored in browser \texttt{localStorage} under the key
\texttt{levitation\_highscores} as a JSON array of
\texttt{\{name, score, np, date\}} objects. The top 7 are displayed.

\section{Viewport System}
\label{sec:viewport}
% =============================================================================

The viewport is a brass-framed screen that slides down from above the drum
on the Microscope or Chart button press. It displays either static image or
video assets, or live canvas-rendered content, driven by a rule engine.

\subsection{Rule Evaluation}

Every $1/5\,\mathrm{s}$, the engine evaluates a bank of rules for the active
channel (\texttt{microscope} or \texttt{chart}). Each rule has:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A \texttt{when} predicate receiving a \texttt{ctx} snapshot of
    simulation state (particle counts, drum state, mode flags, etc.).
  \item An \texttt{asset} (filename string, array for random selection,
    or a \texttt{\{type, src/name\}} descriptor).
  \item A \texttt{priority} (higher wins; default 0).
\end{itemize}

The highest-priority matching rule is selected. Asset changes are crossfaded
over $0.4\,\mathrm{s}$ with a $1.0\,\mathrm{s}$ debounce to prevent flickering.

\subsection{Live Renderers}

Any asset of type \texttt{render} calls a registered JavaScript function
that paints into the viewport's off-screen canvas each frame:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Java]
registerViewportRenderer('myPlot', function(g, w, h, ctx, t) {
    // g: 2D context  w,h: pixel size  ctx: simulation snapshot  t: sim time
});
\end{lstlisting}
The \texttt{ctx} object exposes particle counts, drum state, mode flags, and
current parameters, enabling data-driven visualisations.

\subsection{Context Object Summary}

Key fields available in \texttt{ctx}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{ctx.particles.\{floating, levitated, lost, total, ...\ \}}
  \item \texttt{ctx.aggregates.\{active, levitated, totalFormed\}}
  \item \texttt{ctx.pebbles.\{count\}}
  \item \texttt{ctx.drum.\{omega, period, spinning, direction\}}
  \item \texttt{ctx.params.\{N\_P, V\_T, VT\_SPREAD, DT\_INJECT\}}
  \item \texttt{ctx.mode.\{game, gameLevel, challenge, running\}}
  \item \texttt{ctx.flags.\{laserOn, trailsOn, theme\}}
\end{itemize}

    $500\,\mathrm{ms}$ cooldown. Fires on particle--wall collision when
    $N_P \leq 30$. \\
  Snap &
    9 accelerating clicks (sawtooth transients at $2500$--$4100\,\mathrm{Hz}$,
    stepping $+200\,\mathrm{Hz}$ per click, inter-click gap multiplied by 0.85
    each step). Fires on aggregate formation. \\
  Aggregate merge &
    Soft sine thud descending from $600$ to $220\,\mathrm{Hz}$ as aggregate
    size increases from 20 to 100 monomers. Fires on growth merges. \\
  Crunch &
    500 random sawtooth bursts ($2000$--$5000\,\mathrm{Hz}$, $80\,\mathrm{ms}$
    random offsets, $0.8\,\mathrm{s}$ total). Fires on pebble formation. \\
  Chime &
    Two-note (880 and $1320\,\mathrm{Hz}$) sine arpeggio with $150\,\mathrm{ms}$
    cooldown. Fires when a particle achieves levitation. \\
  Golden chime &
    Four-note arpeggio (C5, E5, G5, D6) with decaying envelope. Fires when
    the aggregate gauge or pebble gauge first appears. \\
  Golden thud &
    Sine sweep $160 \to 70\,\mathrm{Hz}$, amplitude proportional to collision
    speed. Fires on strong golden-ball wall or ball--ball impacts. \\
  Tray beep &
    Single 880\,Hz square-wave pulse ($\approx 350\,\mathrm{ms}$ duration),
    repeated every $1.0\,\mathrm{s}$ while the tray is in the \emph{armed} or
    \emph{inserting} phase. Provides auditory countdown feedback to the operator. \\
  Mill start &
    Full five-component sequence (blade, hiss, grind, thud, body) lasting the
    injection duration. Fires on Inject press. \\
  Expert door &
    Heavy metallic clunk plus a 3-second cavernous low-frequency reverb tail.
    Fires when the expert door is clicked while closed. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{longtable}

The \texttt{AudioContext} is created and suspended on first load. It is
resumed on the first user gesture (\texttt{pointerdown} or \texttt{keydown}),
as required by browser autoplay policies. Muting sets the master gain to 0
with a $50\,\mathrm{ms}$ ramp; the audio graph remains active.
Tab visibility changes also mute and unmute smoothly.

\section{Performance Notes}
% =============================================================================

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Trail rendering} is disabled for $N_P \geq 300$ (the
    \texttt{maxN} tuning constant). At high particle counts, per-particle
    trail segment storage and rendering becomes the bottleneck.

  \item \textbf{Particle glow} (radial gradient per particle) is skipped
    for $N_P \geq 300$; only the core circle is drawn.

  \item \textbf{Injection puffs} are limited to one burst of 5 puffs
    (one per nozzle) at injection start, regardless of $N_P$. Each puff
    generates three \texttt{createRadialGradient} calls per frame for its
    $1.42\,\mathrm{s}$ lifetime. At $N_P = 3000$ over a $2\,\mathrm{s}$
    injection window, the original per-particle puff design created up to
    $\sim 1500\,\mathrm{puffs\,s^{-1}}$, causing significant frame-rate
    drops on mobile WebKit (observed on iPad).

  \item \textbf{Encounter prediction} updates its cache at $\approx 5\,\mathrm{Hz}$
    (every 12 frames at 60 fps) to keep the $O(n^2)$ pairwise computation
    from dominating each frame.

  \item \textbf{Heatmap} finalisation runs once per full drum revolution,
    not every frame; the per-frame cost is only the accumulation of one
    position per particle per substep.
\end{itemize}
